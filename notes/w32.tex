\section{Grid}

Based on the simple binary evolution grids I showed last week, I have decided to simulate a high dimensional grid that looks at a broad mass range, broad initial mass-ratio
range, and initial Roche lobe radii such that I simulate pretty much the complete RLOF regime with some dredge up.

I do this for two RLOF accretion efficiencies, each with three different ratios for isotropic re-emission and circumbinary mass loss.
The ratios are \((\text{CCT}, \text{isotropic re-emission}) = (0.1,0.9)\quad{,}(0.2,0.8)\quad{,}(0.3,0.7)\).
The accretion efficiencies are \(0\) and \(0.1\).

The plot below shows the complete grid.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Notice in naive simple binary case, the different ratios between
		isotropic re-emission and mass loss to a circumbinary torus would result in the same accretion and thus
		in the same abundances.
		Therefore, each dot is really \(3\) dots stacked on top of each other.
	}

	\marginfignote{12}{In the detailed MESA simulation, there will most likely be small differences
		between the final abundances for these systems.
		However, the biggest differences between these
		will be in the final orbital properties of these systems.
		More mass loss to a circumbinary torus
		will generally lead to much tighter final binary systems.
	}

	\marginfignote{28}{The panels with \(\varepsilon_\text{RLOF} = 0.1\) and \(\varepsilon_\text{RLOF} = 0.0\) at first sight might look very similar.
		This is because in both these cases, \emph{most} of the mass is transferred via winds, which are not affected by the efficiency parameter.
		However, in the regime where winds are not very strong, differences between the two settings can be seen.
		This is on the left side of the plot.
		Here we see that the systems with \emph{some} RLOF accretion are generally able to

\newsection{Mean Molecular Weight}

\subsection{Giant Branch Dip}

Using the \lstinline[style=output, breaklines=true]|EOS_plotter| routine from \emph{MESA}, it
is possible to clearly plot the different EOS tables that MESA uses, and in which regions in
the \(\rho\)-\(T\) diagram.
Below, I show this, and I plot the \(\rho\)-\(T\) profile for
an evolved RGB star over these tables.
\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{The RGB profile is colored, where very red regions correspond to regions with relatively large mean molecular weight \(\mu \).
		Blue regions correspond to the lowest mean molecular weights, and white corresponds to an average mean molecular weight of the envelope.
		I think this figure quite clearly shows why we see a pit in the mean molecular weight.
		The core boundary happens inside the Skye EoS table \citep{jermyn2021}.
		This table \emph{assumes} complete ionization.
		Once the profile crosses over to the boundary region, we see that the mean molecular weight increases until it crosses
		the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS} regime, which does \emph{not assume} complete ionization.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-eos}
\end{figure}

Now that the regions of the different equations of state tables are clear, and we know that
our giant branch models cross into the Skye EOS just before the core, we can again make a plot
of the \(\mu \) as a function of depth in the star.
For the sake of clarity, I chose \(\log R\) for
the horizontal axis, but obviously one could chose various different quantities such as internal mass,
temperature or pressure.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{This plot \emph{clearly} shows that the pit in the \(\mu \) profile is not real.
		The Skye EOS \citep{jermyn2021} \emph{assumes} complete ionization, which, according to the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS}, is clearly not
		true up until the deepest parts of its influence.
		The cyan blending region just ensures the two
		different tables do not produce discontinuous profiles.
	}
	\marginfignote{13}{We also clearly see that my naive complete ionization prescription to compute the mean molecular weight perfectly matches the Skye \citep{jermyn2021}
		EOS, and starts to diverge in the blending region, just as I would expect based on the fact that Skye assumes full ionization and FreeEOS does not.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-EOS-profile-RGB}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Main Sequence}

Now, let's look at the main sequence.
Maybe here we see a similar crossing between EOS regimes.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{I once again plot the \(\rho\)-\(T\) plane and the regions of the different
		EOS tables.
		I then plot 3 different MS models with different masses and \(X(\text{H})_\text{center} = 0.65\).
		I also indicate where the hydrogen burning core boundary is with a black dot.
	}
	\marginfignote{12}{The plot clearly shows that the heaviest model here crosses into the Skye EOS \citep{jermyn2021}
		in its core.
		The entirety of the \(1\; M_\odot \) model uses the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS} table and the \(0.4\; M_\odot\) crosses into the OPAL/SCVH region \citep{rogers2002}.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-eos-2}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Again, we see that the mean molecular weight of the Skye EOS agrees perfectly
		with my fully ionized computation.
		As soon as we enter the blended region, the mean molecular
		weight starts to deviate.
	}
	\marginfignote{10}{The weird thing is that the \citet{asplund2009} mean molecular weight agrees with the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS} nearly perfectly.}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-mu-2}
\end{figure}

It took me an embarrassingly long time, but I \emph{think} I have finally been able to figure out what causes the differences in \(\mu \) between the Skye EOS and FreeEOS.
The differences can be explained by the masses of the elements that are used in the computation of \(\mu \).
My naive approach for complete ionization for the
\emph{MESA} abundances was to simply take integer mass numbers equal to the number of protons plus neutrons.
\emph{However}, for the \citet{asplund2009} mean molecular weight
computations, I use the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_atomic_weight#cite_note-23}{Standard atomic weight}.
This is a bit different.

It seems like Skye \citep{jermyn2021} uses the mass number, while the FreeEOS uses the atomic mass.
In fact, when I compute the \emph{MESA} mean molecular weight
using both methods, we can clearly see that the computed mean molecular weights match depending on which mass is used.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Clearly, the ``mass number'' mean molecular weights match the Skye \citep{jermyn2021}
		EOS mean molecular weights.
		The ``atomic mass'' mean molecular weights match the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS} mean molecular weights.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-mu-3}
\end{figure}

This is not necessarily a problem.
For all main sequence models, the blending region, if it even exists, is \emph{inside} the core.
This means that for the entire mixing region, the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net}{FreeEOS} mean molecular weight is used.
I use this same ``atomic mass'' prescription to compute \(\mu \) for the accreted material, so to compute the
mixing mass, we can simply use the full ionization with atomic masses.
There are then no inconsistencies.

I have now implemented the fully ionized mean molecular weight computation into the accretor code.
Since it agrees so well with the normal \emph{MESA} EOS, there are no noticeable differences
in the abundances or mixing masses.

Let me first focus on the set of models with the third Roche lobe radius.
These are interesting because
they interact during the phase of strong winds.
We can recognise this in the figure above by the clear
upwards diagonal in the \(\varepsilon = 0\) models.
The downwards sloping diagonal line that follows it
contains models that interact during the last thermal pulse.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Here, I plot the Roche lobe radius and the star radius as a function of time
		for the models with an initial Roche lobe radius of approximately \(R_\text{RL} = 520\; R_\odot \).
		The most obvious differences between the models is caused by the differences in \(f_\delta \), which is to be expected.
		We see that more mass loss to a CCT leads to smaller final periods.
		A second, initially less obvious effect is that these models interact \emph{earlier}.
		This effects how much mass is lost through \emph{winds}.
	}
	\marginfignote{18}{If we look at the total amount of accreted mass, we can clearly explain the differences we see between the simple binary and the detailed \emph{MESA} simulations.
		For the \(\varepsilon =0\) case, the earlier RLOF interaction causes less mass to be transferred via winds for the \(f_\delta =0.3\) model.
		For the \(f_{\delta } = 0.1 \) model, there are still significant winds during and after RLOF, which invalidates the assumption of the simple binary that all mass is lost through RLOF once this starts.
		For the \(\varepsilon =0.1\) models, this effect causes the differences.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-radius-3}
\end{figure}

Now, let me look at the first Roche lobe radius, where we see some peculiar differences, especially for the \(\varepsilon =0, f_\delta =0.1\) model.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{-3}{Apparently, these different mass transfer mechanisms cause these models to interact
		during different thermal pulses.
		This makes it slightly confusing to interpret the differences between
		the simple binary and the detailed \emph{MESA} models.
		To start, we see that the \(f_{\delta } = 0.3 \) models interact first.
		This means
		these models will transfer mass, independent of how much, with the \emph{lowest} enrichment.
		In fact, the envelope is barely enriched at all during this thermal pulse and therefore, both
		of these models do not show any enhanced [s/Fe].
		Then, the orange \(f_{\delta } = 0.2 \), models.
		These interact one pulse later, but still earlier than the simple binary.
		This means that these models will have lower, but still slightly enhanced envelope abundances, which explains why the model with \(\varepsilon =0.1\) predicts a slightly enhanced [s/Fe], but still lower than the simple binary.
		Lastly, we have the \(f_{\delta } = 0.1 \) models.
		These undergo RLOF during the same thermal pulse, \emph{but} they do not expel their entire
		envelope during RLOF, unlike what we assume for the simple binary.
		Because the envelope becomes enriched due to dredge up \emph{after} the RLOF episode, wind mass losses after
		this episode are especially important.
		Because the \(\varepsilon =0.0\) model transfers no not very enriched material during RLOF, and about the same amount of enhanced material with winds, it predicts the highest [s/Fe] value.
	}
	\marginfignote{49}{Let's look at this a bit more carefully.
		The second and third panel show the amount of accreted mass, and the amount of accreted s-process mass.
		Clearly these are both larger for the \(\varepsilon =0.1\) model, which makes sense, because it is able to transfer mass during RLOF.
	}
	\marginfignote{60}{The subplot on the left shows the averaged abundance of the material that is accreted.
		It is important to realise that this is \emph{not} the
		instantaneous abundance of the material that is accreted at time \(t\).
		Because a strong third dredge up episode enhances the envelope of the TPAGB star after RLOF
		in the blue models, the abundances of s-process elements are much larger after this dredge up.
		This means the averaged abundance of the accreted material is much larger for the \(\varepsilon =0\) model than for the \(\varepsilon =0.1\) model.
	}
	\marginfignote{80}{We can then accrete this material onto the accretor.
		In this case I use a constant mixing mass that is determined based on the final value, so \emph{strictly speaking}, only the last value is accurate.
		The subplot to the left and the one above it show the s-process abundances based on this assumption.
		The first being \emph{no} initial \(s\)-process elements in the accretor, and the second using more realistic initial s-process abundances based on \citet{asplund2009}.
		Without initial abundances, the \(\varepsilon =0.1\) model generates the largest accretor abundance, simply because it accretes more s-process elements.
		However, because the bulk of this accreted material has abundances that are very close to the initial value, these really do not contribute much if we consider an accretor with initial s-process abundances.
	}
	\marginfignote{110}{Finally, if we use the correct mass-fractions, we can compute [s/Fe] for the models, and we obtain the final values.}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-radius-4}
\end{figure}

I feel like, with this, I can explain the differences between the simple binary and the detailed \emph{MESA} models.
They originate from different RLOF moments and the assumption
of complete RLOF mass transfer.

To start, let me plot the [s/Fe] abundances as a function of the final period of the binary.

\begin{figure}[ht]

I think these systems are a combination of two effects.
First, we have that
the \(\delta =0.3\) models have much stronger orbital shrinkage, which moves them
to lower periods from the right.
So the clustered systems on the upper right of the plot
migrate to \emph{lower periods} when they have a higher \(\delta \).

Then secondly, we have the effect where systems with lower \(q\) respond more intensely to mass loss,
and therefore obtain much shorter periods and in many cases also lower abundances.

We can confirm this by using \(q\) as the color instead of the initial Roche lobe radius.

\begin{figure}[ht]

		or both.

\end{figure}
