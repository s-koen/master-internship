The interactive figure above seems to imply that the superadiabatic region grows during the TPAGB.

\href{https://s-koen.github.io/figures/#/plots/master-internship/w21/super-ad}{This interactive figure} tries to show this. The region grows quite significantly with time. This could have the effect that it is not necessarily correct to assume the superadiabatic surface layers contribue so little to the amount of transferred mass that its contribution can be ignored and the constant envelope abundance can be used.

\newsection{AGB-Nucleosynthesis}
I think \citet{karakas2016} provide abundance data for every thermal pulse for a grid of initial masses and metallicities.

Unfortunately, the metal-poor star they provide has \(z=0.007\), so I cannot trivially match it with a MESA model. 
For the solar metallicity stars, I \emph{can} do this. 
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{The C/O-ratios do not align \emph{at all}. Not only does the MESA model experience way fewer pulses, it also starts to enrich its envelope much earlier, and the rate at which it enriches its envelope is much faster too.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-agb-nucleosynthis-compare-mesa}
\end{figure}

I think this is quite concerning. It does not seem like we can easily match my MESA model to a Monash model from \citet{karakas2016}. The only thing that is truely consistent is the starting C/O-ratio, but this is expected as both models use the \citet{asplund2009} solar model.

The core masses are also quite different.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{The most notable difference is obviously that the core mass keeps growing for the \citet{karakas2016} models long after the MESA model has lost its envelope.}

    \centering
    \incplot{w21-agb-nucleosynthis-compare-core-mass}
\end{figure}

An alternative could be using the NuGrid data.

As a first test, I plot the He-core mass as a function of age

\begin{figure}[ht]

Apart from the core mass, the mass loss rate is very different too.

\newsection{Constant Mass-loss Rates}

I an attempt to better understand the radial response of the TPAGB star to mass loss, I have simulated a number of models with various \emph{constant} mass loss rates ranging from \(10^{-3}\;M_\odot / \text{yr}\) to \(10^{-0}\;M_\odot / \text{yr}\). The models losing more than \(10^{-1}\;M_\odot / \text{yr}\) however, failed.

Below I plot the results.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{We see a similar behaviour as for the detailed binary simulations, where the models with the most extreme mass ratio, and thus the strongest mass loss rate would dip before reaching the post-AGB sequence.}
    \marginfignote{12}{There are minor differences though, in particular, the models with a constant mass loss rate do not seem to decrease their radius quite as much as the models with variable mass loss rate.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-constant-mass-loss-rate-radius}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{The HR diagram shows the move to lower luminosities clearly.}
    \marginfignote{9}{There is also very clearly something weird going on towards the end of the evolution, where the stars start to move to higher luminosities. I suspect this might have something to do with the large mass-loss rates being sustained when the envelope becomes \emph{very} thin.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-constant-mass-loss-rate-HR}
\end{figure}

For these models, I saved profiles logarithmically spaced in envelope mass. This allows for some more detailed inspection of the stellar interior although I am not sure if this was really very helpful. 
Below I tried to summarize my findings.

The temperature gradient does not change significantly with mass loss rate.

The same is true for the thermal time to the surface.

The same is true for the mixing and nuclear burning.

The same is true for the pressure as well as the individual components of the pressure.

The same is true for the temperature.

\href{https://s-koen.github.io/figures/#/plots/master-internship/w21/losing-envelope-opacity}{The opacity is larger in the envelope of models that lose mass more quickly.}

\href{https://s-koen.github.io/figures/#/plots/master-internship/w21/losing-envelope-density}{The density is larger in the envelope of models that lose mass more quickly.}
\href{https://s-koen.github.io/figures/#/plots/master-internship/w21/losing-envelope-entropy}{The entropy stays a bit more flat.}

\href{https://s-koen.github.io/figures/#/plots/master-internship/w21/losing-envelope-entropy}{But most significantly, the gravitational luminosity changes a lot for the different models.}

\section{Conservation Grid}

\subsection{Varying \texorpdfstring{\(\beta \)}{beta}}
I ran some simulation of a single binary model with different assumptions regarding the conservation of mass transfer. All models start with an initial Roche lobe radius of about \(R_\text{RL} \approx 550\;R_\odot\) and \(q=0.5\). I used \(\gamma =1.44\) for the models to simulate \(L_{2}\)-outflows.

Let me start by showing the effect of \(\beta \) for models with a constant value of \(\delta =0.3\).
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{The effect on the star radius is minimal, while a larger \(\beta \) tends to result in somewhat smaller final Roche lobe radii. This corresponds to shorter periods too, which I show in the figure below.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-conservation-grid-beta-r}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{This shows that the periods are shorter for models with larger \(\beta \). This is also what I expect from the analytical results from \citet{soberman1997}.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-conservation-grid-beta-periods}
\end{figure}

Since the radius of the star barely seems to change for different values of \(\beta \), I do not expect the post-AGB approach to differ.

In the figure below I plot the HR diagram for these models.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{Clearly, there is very little difference in how the star behaves.}
    \centering
    \incplot{w21-conservation-grid-beta-HR}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Varying \texorpdfstring{\(\delta \)}{delta}}
Perhaps more interesting is to see the effects of \(\delta \). In this case, I change \(\delta \) and set \(\beta \) such that \(\delta + \beta = 1\)\sidenote{So no accretion.}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \marginfignote{3}{Clearly, changing \(\delta \) has a much larger effect than \(\beta \).}
    \marginfignote{7}{Some models stop because the binary spirals in. I have not plotted these.}
    \marginfignote{13}{Some models only ran until \(M_\text{env} = 0.01M_\odot \) because I forgot to remove the MESA default envelope mass stopping condition. The models are removing clearly behave as expected, stopping only once RLOF has well and truely stopped.}

    \incplot{w21-conservation-grid-delta-r}

\begin{figure}[ht]
