\section{Thermohaline mixing}

Towards the end of the giant branch, we have a mean molecular weight transition between the Skye EOS \citep{jermyn2021} and
the \href{https://freeeos.sourceforge.net/}{FreeEOS} tables which occurs in a radiative part of the star.
This transition causes a \emph{higher} mean molecular weight region to be \emph{above} a lower mean
molecular weight region.
Even though this mean molecular weight transition is unphysical and
entirely caused by the differences in the atom masses used to calculate \(\mu \) for both tables,
\emph{MESA} should detect this transition as a region which is unstable to thermohaline (TH) mixing.

In the plot below, I show the \(\mu \) profiles, together with the EOS tables and the mixing regions for a
star at the tip of the RGB.
\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{We can quite clearly see that MESA recognises the thermohaline instability and applies
		mixing in this region.
		The region is just above the transition from Skye to FreeEOS.
	}
	\marginfignote{9}{I tested a rotating model too, to see if the thermohaline mixing would be significantly
		affected by the rotation, however, both models, even though they do experience thermohaline mixing, maintain
		their \(\mu \) dip.}
	\marginfignote{20}{This makes a lot of sense if we think about how thermohaline mixing works and how we would
		usually expect the \(\mu \) profile to change due to TH mixing.
		Usually, the instability occurs due to
		different compositions in different layers.
		TH mixing then mixes these compositions, which in turn
		equalized the mean molecular weight profile.
		However, in this case, the composition is actually nearly
		identical throughout the mixing region, and even if we completely mix the otherwise unmixed region, the
		composition stays the same, and we maintain the \(\mu \) profile dip due to the differences in the
		opacity tables.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w33-th-2}
\end{figure}

However, for nitrogen and carbon, there are some small differences in the composition in the thermohaline
mixing regime.
This is a nice test-case to see the effects that ``fake'' thermohaline mixing can have on the
composition of the star.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{In this case, we can compare the composition of the RGB model with and without thermohaline
		mixing and rotation.
		We see that the composition boundary becomes ``smoothed'' due to the thermohaline mixing,
		which is what we expect.
		However, the thermohaline mixing should not even exist here so this effect is
		actually not physical and wrong.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w33-TH-3}
\end{figure}

\newsection{Mixing mass and the Effect on the final abundances}

Last week, I showed this plot:
\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{1}{Every plot here makes some intuitive sense if we consider that the envelope
		s-process abundances increase after thermal pulses up to the point were we start actually \emph{accreting} material
		onto a main sequence star, and we obtain MS abundances.}
	\marginfignote{65}{This is where things start to become a little difficult to predict.
		This panel shows the abundances in a MS accretor if the MS itself has no initial s-process abundances.
		To understand these abundances, we need to consider both the mixing mass, \emph{and} the accreted mass.
	}
	\marginfignote{83}{For the actual main sequence abundances, it is important to also consider the initial abundances of the s-process elements in the main sequence star.}
	\centering
	\incplot{w32-radius-4}
\end{figure}

Let me plot the \(\mu\)-mixing profiles for the blue models (\(f_{\delta } = 0.1\)).
That way, we can see
how deep the thermohaline mixing should extend, and what the differences are between the two.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{The orange line has less accretion, but with higher s-process abundances, and thus a higher mean molecular weight.
		The lower accretion mass is more important in this case.
		The \(\varepsilon =0.0\) model has a lower mixing mass.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w33-mixing-mass-1}
\end{figure}

To confirm the mixing mass actually fixes MS abundances, let me show, explicitly,
a calculation of the final accretor abundances.
First, let's consider the
case \emph{without} any initial abundances in the accretor.
Then we simply have

\begin{align*}
	X_\text{MS}                       & = \Delta M_\text{s-process} / (M_\text{mix} + \Delta M_\text{accr})        \\
	X_\text{MS, \(\varepsilon =0.1\)} & \approx 9 \times  10^{-9} / (0.09 + 0.464) \approx 1.62 \times  10^{-8}    \\
	X_\text{MS, \(\varepsilon =0.0\)} & \approx 5.5 \times  10^{-9} / (0.015 + 0.359) \approx 1.47 \times  10^{-8}
\end{align*}
These values, although approximate, align very well with what we see in the subplot showing \(\langle X \rangle (s\text{, MS} )_{t=t}, X(s,i) = 0 \)

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Here I plot the main sequence abundance as a function of the mixing
		mass.
		We can clearly see that, in the limit of \(M_\text{mix}\to 0\), \(X_\text{MS}(s) = X_\text{accr.
			mat.}(s)\).
		Since the \emph{abundances} of the accreted material in the \(\varepsilon =0.0\) case are higher, we end up with larger final
		abundances in the MS star too.
		Then, as we go to larger mixing masses, there comes a crossing point where the \emph{quantity} of the s-process elements
		is just as important as the proportion of s-process elements in the accreted material.
		Then, towards even higher masses, we reach the actual mixing masses and we can see that, even though the \(\varepsilon =0.1\) model has a deeper mixing mass, it still produces a larger final accretor abundance.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w33-mixing-mass-vs-X-no-initial}
\end{figure}

Now, if we consider the initial abundances of the accretor, I calculate the abundances as follows:
\begin{align*}
	X_\text{MS}                       & = (\Delta M_\text{s-process} + X(s) \cdot M_\text{mix}) / (M_\text{mix} + \Delta M_\text{accr})              \\
	X_\text{MS, \(\varepsilon =0.1\)} & \approx (9 \times  10^{-9} + 4.4 \times 10^{-8} \cdot 0.464 ) / (0.09 + 0.464) \approx 5.31 \times  10^{-8}  \\
	X_\text{MS, \(\varepsilon =0.0\)} & \approx (5.5 \times  10^{-9}+ 4.4 \times 10^{-8} \cdot 0.359) / (0.015 + 0.359) \approx 5.69 \times  10^{-8}
\end{align*}
Both of these values align very well with the plot showing \(\langle X \rangle (s\text{, MS} )_{t=t},\)\\\(X(s,i) = X(s)_\text{Asplund}\).

Again, we can plot the effect of the mixing mass.
\begin{figure}[ht]
	\marginfignote{0}{Again, we see that, in the limit of \(M_\text{mix}\to 0\), \(X_\text{MS}(s) = X_\text{accr. mat.}(s)\).
		This time however, as we increase the mixing mass, we approach the initial abundance of the main sequence accretor.
		The lines never cross.
		The \(\varepsilon =0.0\) model has a lower mixing mass, and a larger accretor abunance, even though it accreted less material.
	}
	\centering
	\incplot{w33-mixing-mass-vs-X}
\end{figure}
