\newsection{MESA intershell abundances}

In order to verify the Monash enrichment is working well, we can compare the enrichment of the envelope as predicted by my Monash post-processing code with the abundances from MESA.

I think there are a couple interesting things to do. First, we just want to compare the intershell abundances of the elements that are available to me in the MESA models with the
predicted Monash intershell abundances. This is already interesting, because this gives us a good idea of it the interpolation is working well.

Then, I think it is interesting to look at the envelope abundances to compare the differences. I have already done this previously, \emph{but} now I have a ``better'' initial envelope abundance, and insight into the intershell abundances of both codes.

Lastly, I want to verify that my envelope enrichment code is working correctly. I will do this by injecting the MESA intershell abundances and the initial envelope abundance into
my post-processor. I will then compare the envelope abundances it spits out with the actual MESA envelope abundances. If the post-processor is working well, it should reproduce the
MESA envelope abundances well\sidenote{Although I do not expect it to agree completely, because the dredge up assumed to be instantaneous, and the precise derived dredged up masses
are slightly different from the MESA internal dredged-up masses.}.

\newsection{Investigating Monash Yields}
% why are the mesa abundances structurally about a factor 2 larger? and why is there a difference between elements before say element pr

First things first, I made an error last week which quite drastically changed the results for the yields.
However, the other results where all unaffected. The problem was in my implementation of the post-processor
when it just uses the single star model. In this case, the post-processor does not have the parameters dict
that the binary models do contain and the post-processor has to infer some of the relevant information itself.

Most importantly, it has to find the mass and the metallicity of the star. Now, I thought I would simply use
the star property information for this, \emph{but} apparently I had hardcoded a solar metallicity into the
star model, which caused the post-processor to wrongly identify the models as solar metallicity models.
It then used this metallicity to compute the interpolation and obtain the initial envelope abundances.

\begin{figure}[ht]

I think the interesting isotope here might be \(^{93}\text{Zr} \), which has a long halflife, but it decays to a stable isotope of our underrepresented element.
It is a bit difficult to look at the \emph{envelope} abundances of \(^{93}\text{Zr}\), because we only have information on the \emph{element} Zr, not the individual
isotopes.

In the paper from \citet{karakas2016}, they write the following about \(^{93}\text{Zr}\):
\begin{shadequote}{\citet{karakas2016}}
Note that we do not decay
the abundances of radioactive isotopes (e.g.,  \(^{26}\)Al, \(^{60}\)Fe)
in the isotopic or elemental surface abundance files, but
they are assumed to have all decayed in the yield tables.
However, we do decay the isotope \(^{93}\)Zr to \(^{93}\)Nb because
Nb obtains essentially all of its production via this decay.
\end{shadequote}

I think I can do the same, but I am not quite sure why they do this. The half-life time
is about 1.6 million years and this, at least to to the same order of magnitude, about the same
duration of the TPAGB phase of stars with masses between \(1.5\; M_\odot \) and \(3.0\; M_\odot \).

actually simulating the decay
\emph{in the envelope}. For this, I assume that
\(^{93}\text{Zr}\) has \emph{no} initial abundance in the envelope, since it is an unstable isotope.

The yield fits a lot worse this time. With the previous method, I would say that the differences
between the individual Monash models are bigger than the differences between the \emph{MESA} model and the

There are however a few other elements that are
still a bit ``weird''. With the exception of Cs, these are all elements that quite significantly
underrepresented in the MESA yields. I think here the story is similar to the story of Nb.
There is some isotope that decays to the underrepresented element, probably with quite a long decay time.

For these isotopes, I could implement a similar procedure. This however requires a lot of manual work, or
quite a bit of setup work to load some nuclides table, compare every isotope with this decay process
in the table and implement the correct decay.

I think my interpolation probably introduces more uncertainty than the reactions that happen in the envelope
or in the interstellar medium after the mass has been expelled.

However, this problem is obviously not only there for the yields, but also for the envelope abundances as a
function of time, which is easy to see if we look at the \(^{93}\text{Zr}\) abundance plot above.

The last thing I want to investigate is the effects of the total expelled mass.

Naively, one would expect that models with the same initial mass would expel roughly
the same amount of mass.
\emph{However}, differences in the core mass do change the total amount a little bit.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering

These differences however are so small that after "correcting" for this, the abundances
distributions changes are minimal.

We see that the especially for s-process elements, the MESA model predicts slightly
\emph{higher} yields than both the Monash models. I think this makes sense if we take into
account the fact that the MESA models often experience the first-dredge up \emph{earlier}.
This means the stars will have more thermal pulses to dredge-up enriched intershell material
to the envelope. If the star has higher envelope abundances at the end of the evolution when
mass losses become important, we will obviously see \emph{larger} yields for these elements.
